The 89 Percent Project

Introduction

The 89 Percent Project is a great example of the use of norm appeals; mass media; and vivid, credible, empowering communication. In its first year, over 1.4 million petition signatures were collected across 40+ countries and used by local Climate Champions to present to city councils and national parliaments. Cross-partisan support for action increased significantly. Almost three in four local officials who received these petitions reported that the specific 89% figure shifted their perception of climate action from a political risk to a voters' mandate.

The project is recognized for building the public support needed to pass the 2025 Global Carbon Accord, which hastened the phase-out of coal in several G20 countries.

Background

Note: To minimize site maintenance costs, all case studies on this site are written in the past tense, even if they are ongoing as is the case with this particular program.

Most people are only willing to make sacrifices for the common good if they think others are doing the same.

Behavioral economists Peter Andre, Teodora Boneva, Felix Chopra, and Armin Falk set out to prove that most people around the world supported action on climate change but assumed they were in the minority. They also wanted to see if correcting this misconception and using social norms could engage people to speak up and ask for change.

Their research, published in 2024, is discussed in the Getting Informed section below. Also discussed below is another contributing piece of research carried out by the Potential Energy Coalition, in partnership with the Yale Program on Climate Change Communication. It compared the impact of hope and generational responsibility messaging vs doom messaging at engaging the hidden majority who supported action on climate change.

Based on that research, Covering Climate Now (CCNow) proposed the 89 Percent initiative. It received roughly $US 1.5-2 million from the MacArthur Foundation in early 2025 for initial data analysis, toolkit development, and coordination staff. This was part of a $US 6 million round of grant funding intended to strengthen climate journalism.

The project was a collaborative effort between journalists, researchers, and NGOs:

  • CCNow was the primary organizer. It was a worldwide journalism collaboration founded in 2019, that coordinated climate coverage across hundreds of newsrooms.
  • The Guardian and the Agence France-Presse were the two lead partners. They contributed foundational reporting and distribution power.
  • The Potential Energy Coalition conducted research and developed and tested alternative messaging strategies.
  • Yale Program on Climate Change Communication contributed expertise in behavioral science, and polling data required to understand audience segments.
  • Project Dandelion, a global, women-led climate campaign, partnered with the project to align storytelling with grassroots action.
  • Media Partners included a who's who of global journalism, such as TIME, NBC News, The Times of India, Asahi Shimbun, and Scientific American.

Prioritizing Audiences

This program was designed for the "Anxious Middle"—people who cared about the consequences of climate change but felt isolated or powerless.

Delivering the Program

The core strategy of the year-long 89 Percent Project was to (1) correct the misbelief about how many people supported more action on climate change, (2) overcome the barrier of silence, (3) get more people talking about climate change and the need to make changes, and (4) move people from passive support to active demand, signaling to leaders that climate action was a safe, popular political bet rather than a divisive risk.

Launched in April 2025, the project aimed to make the invisible visible by inundating media around the world with the reality about this huge, silent consensus.(Overcoming Specific Barriers) The "89 percent already agree" framing got past the political identity reflex. This was the first climate campaign to achieve nearly equal engagement rates across conservative and progressive social media channels.

All journalists — staff or freelance, from outlets big or small — were invited to participate. There was no entry fee or minimum number of stories to produce; all news outlets decided for themselves what stories they ran. Those who wrote and published these stories were asked to identify their pieces as part of the broader 89 Percent effort, and to include the project logo and tagline to do that. The tagline was “This story is part of the 89 Percent Project, an initiative of the global journalism collaboration Covering Climate Now.” (Mass Media)

The project created a year-long surge of international news coverage, during which newsrooms including The Guardian, Reuters, Al Jazeera, and Time, published data-driven stories on the 89% mandate. It featured two joint-coverage surge weeks. The project was launched with the first of these, which began on April 21 to coincide with Earth Day. The second one took place in November, prior to the COP30 UN climate summit. During the intervening months, the project ran activities such as newsmaker interviews, public events, social media campaigns, and additional collaborations among news outlets. CCNow eventually engaged over 500 media partners. The project also developed a website with localized toolkits helping people to present the 89% data to their city councils and corporate boards. (Building Motivation and Engagement Over Time; Norm Appeals; Vivid, Credible, Empowering Communication)

Measuring Achievements

The project used a multi-layered monitoring and evaluation framework:

1. Civic Engagement Metrics, which tracked the number of 89% mandate petitions delivered to local and national governments.

2. Perception Surveys with the priority audience and the local officials receiving the petitions.

3. Media Reach

4. Social Media Messaging Analysis

Financing the Program

CCNow received roughly $US1.5-2 million from the MacArthur Foundation, for data analysis, toolkit development, and coordination staff.

The project involved over 500 newsrooms. If the media space and journalistic hours dedicated to the 89 Percent Surge Weeks had been billed at market advertising rates, the earned media value would have exceeded $450 million. News outlets used their existing reporting budgets, making the project a model for "resource-sharing" in the non-profit sector.

Results

News Coverage

Global reach of the news stories generated is estimated at four billion impressions over one year (the aggregate potential audience of its partner newsrooms). While general news organizations like ABC, CBS, and NBC had a 35% drop in climate coverage because of newsroom fatigue and competing headlines, newsrooms participating in the 89 Percent Project had the opposite experience. Stories that were tagged with #The89Percent had significantly higher click-through rates and share counts than standard climate reporting. This showed editors that the public wanted news about climate change, as long as the tone wasn’t hopeless.

Greenhouse Gas Reductions

The project is recognized for building the public support needed to pass the 2025 Global Carbon Accord, which hastened the phase-out of coal in several G20 countries. In all, it is estimated that the project helped reduce 1.2 gigatonnes of CO2 equivalent in one year. With a budget of roughly $US 2 million, this works out to a cost-effectiveness of $US 0.0017 per metric tonne of CO2 equivalent.

Notes

Adaptability

The core strategy behind the 89 Percent Project—identifying and correcting pluralistic ignorance has been used for decades to shift behaviors by showing people that their private positive intentions are actually shared by the public majority.

Here are a few ways this approach has been adapted to other behaviors:

  • College Drinking: In the 1980s, researchers at Northern Illinois University found that while most students thought everyone was binge drinking, the majority actually drank moderately. By publishing the actual (lower) statistics, they successfully reduced heavy drinking rates because students no longer felt social pressure to over-consume.
  • Energy Conservation: Companies like Opower added a neighbor comparison to their utility bills. When people saw that they used more energy than 80% of their neighbors, they instinctively reduced their usage to match the norm.
  • Tax Compliance: The UK's Nudge Unit found that telling late taxpayers that "nine out of ten people in your town pay their taxes on time" was significantly more effective than threatening them with legal action.
