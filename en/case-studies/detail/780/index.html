Empowered Schools

Introduction

The Empowered Schools program transforms school buildings from being a passive administrative overhead cost into active, hands-on learning laboratories. By engaging students, educators, and facilities staff, the program fostered a local culture of sustainability while yielding immediate financial and environmental relief for school districts.

Background

Note: To minimize site maintenance costs, all case studies on this site are written in the past tense, even if they are ongoing as is the case with this particular program.

Energy consumption was the second-largest operational expenditure for K-12 school districts in the United States, exceeded only by personnel costs, with aggregate spending of around $8 billion annually. Recognizing that a significant portion of this expenditure was driven by systemic energy waste rather than fixed operational needs, the Alliance to Save Energy (ASE) launched its flagship K-12 behavioral energy efficiency initiative in 1996.

Initially introduced as the Green Schools program and later rebranded as PowerSave Schools, the initiative later underwent a comprehensive digital and structural modernization and become EmPowered Schools.

Prioritizing Audiences

The program was designed for teachers, students and custodial/facilities personnel in U.S. schools from pre-kindergarten to grade 12.

Delivering the Program

The strategic framework of PowerSave and EmPowered Schools was designed around the psychological concepts of norm appeals and distributed ownership. Instead of hiring external consultants to police energy use, the program shifted accountability to the building’s primary daily occupants: the students. (Norm Appeals)

The abstract concept of "saving the planet" was replaced with tangible, immediate rewards—such as school-wide recognition and trophies. Students could earn digital badges. Schools might share some of the financial savings and/or have more left for school activities.

The program was designed to foster energy conservation and load shifting behaviors in schools and in students’ homes. Students were given home energy audit checklists, encouraging them to implement the exact same energy-saving approaches in their households (e.g. using prompts and turning off phantom loads). (Building Motivation and Engagement Over Time; School Programs that Impact the Home; Vivid, Credible, Empowering Communication).

The EmPowered Schools Program had three primary goals:

  • To help schools save money on energy costs and protect the environment through cooperative, school-wide changes in behavior, operations, and maintenance procedures and retrofits of more efficient equipment.
  • To provide hands-on learning opportunities for students.
  • To build pathways to green jobs.

The core strategic pillars included:

  • The Student-Led Delivery Model: Students formed dedicated Energy Teams or Green Teams. This framework relied on peer-to-peer and student-to-teacher accountability, making it socially desirable to practice conservation.
  • Cross-Functional Cooperation: The strategy required explicit alignment between three historically siloed school groups: students, instructional faculty, and custodial/facilities personnel.
  • Utility Co-Sponsorship: To ensure financial sustainability, the program strategically partnered with regional investor-owned utilities (such as PSE&G, New Jersey Natural Gas, and Xcel Energy). Utilities subsidized program implementation as a cost-effective method to meet their state-mandated demand-side management and energy reduction targets.

The program focused on a checklist of actionable, non-divisible behaviors that students could directly control:

  • Turning off classroom lights when leaving for lunch or recess
  • Shutting down computer labs and smartboards every afternoon, and other load-shifting actions
  • Lowering window blinds at the end of the day to reduce thermal loss/gain
  • Unplugging specific non-essential electronics (phantom loads) before holiday breaks

Program implementation followed a structured, multi-phase roadmap over the course of an academic school year.

  • Faculty and Stakeholder Onboarding: Teachers received specialized training and access to standardized, tier-aligned STEM lesson plans. Facilities managers shared historical utility data baselines.
  • Deployment of Diagnostic Toolkits and School Audits: Schools were equipped with physical diagnostic kits, at no cost to participating schools, containing the following items.
    • Lux (light) meters to audit classroom lighting levels and determine if spaces were over-illuminated
    • Digital watt meters to measure phantom power loads from appliances and electronics
    • Infrared temperature sensors to safely detect drafts, insulation gaps, and HVAC inefficiencies
    • Drip gauges and water flow-rate bags to audit water waste in school restrooms and cafeterias (these were a more recent addition to the kit)

    Students used these tools to audits their classrooms, cafeterias, and common areas. When students saw their peers carrying lux meters and auditing classrooms, saving energy stopped being an abstract administrative imposition and became a visible group norm. (Norm Appeals)

  • Student Energy Team Mobilization & Baseline Mapping: At the start of each school year, teachers guided students to form their core Energy Teams. Students established their school’s identity on the national portal and audited their building's baseline footprint.

  • Help from the Portal: As the program evolved into EmPowered Schools, implementation shifted away from physical guides to a centralized, cloud-based educational portal. This platform gamified the process, allowing students to log audit results digitally, watch animated modules on demand response, participate in quizzes, and track their school's progress in real time against national cohorts. (Building Motivation and Engagement Over Time; Feedback; Norm Appeals)

    Instead of students having to manually graph their audit data on paper, the portal automatically aggregated their inputs. It calculated the school's initial state of energy waste and visualized it on a digital dashboard, creating an instant, clear baseline before the school-wide student energy campaigns began. (Overcoming Specific Barriers; Vivid, Personalized, Credible, Empowering Communication)

    In some schools, the local utility sponsor had installed advanced metering infrastructure and could share aggregated interval energy data with the program. The data were automatically fed into the platform, allowing students to look at actual, real-time kWh charts of their school building’s energy use. They could see exactly how much energy the school used overnight or on weekends when it should be asleep.

  • School-Wide Campaigns: Energy Teams ran school-wide interventions. These included Friday Power Downs ahead of weekends, and participation in intra-district energy saving competitions. Students installed visual prompts such as Friday Power-Down checklists hanging on the back of classroom doors; bright, student-made tags attached to appliances that needed to be unplugged before long weekends; and light switch reminders.(Norm Appeals; Prompts)
  • Coordinated National Campaigns: Throughout the school year, ASE coordinated synchronized, nationwide behavioral action windows. For example, during the Winter Break Shutdown campaign, thousands of students across multiple states used identical checklists to pull plug loads, lower thermostats, and secure buildings simultaneously. This collective timing transformed local chores into a nationwide movement, heavily reinforcing social norms. (Norm Appeals)
  • Macro-Level Recognition and Verification: At the close of the academic year, utility bills were collected and weather normalized. The digital portal calculated the final metrics, and ASE announced regional and national winners. Highly visible recognition—such as the SEEA Luminary Award and national press features—elevated the status of the winning schools, solidifying the behavior change into a permanent mark of institutional pride. (Norm Appeals)

The Leaderboard Effect

The program employed multi-school and multi-district competitions to create a broader social norm: Winning schools weren't just recognized locally; they were celebrated on national platforms by the ASE. This further elevated the perceived status of energy-saving behaviors.

Student and Family Pledges

Even after the program went digital, entire classrooms signed public energy pledges that were hung in the hallways for everyone to see. When students, teachers, and custodians walked down the halls and saw these, the school’s energy conservation norm was reinforced. School principals and custodial staff were asked for verbal and written buy-in for turning energy efficiency into an official, shared pact. (Norm Appeals; Obtaining a Commitment)

While these physical pledges handled the internal culture of the school, electronic pledges (e-pledges) served three additional, vital roles.

  • Influencing the Home: E-prompts allowed students to easily take their missions home. They used QR codes or digital links to ask their parents, extended family, and neighbors to sign online pledges committing to specific home energy-saving behaviors (like washing clothes in cold water or unplugging phantom power loads). (Obtaining a Commitment; School Programs that Influence the Home)
  • Frictionless Data Tracking for Utilities: E-pledges allowed for instant aggregation of data into a dashboard. Utility sponsors could easily track how many households in their district had pledged, which actions they took, and the calculated, scalable energy reductions achieved through behavior change. This information could then be cross-referenced with changes in regional utility billing data.
  • Gamification and District Competitions: E-pledges fed into live, digital leaderboards. Classrooms, schools, and even entire school districts competed in energy challenges to see who could secure the most digital pledges from their community. This spiked engagement significantly without creating massive paper waste. (School Programs that Influence the Home)

Load Shifting

Empowered Schools taught students that when you use energy can matter just as much as how much you use.

  • Visualizing the Grid Traffic Jam: Students learned that the electrical grid is like a highway. During rush hour, the highway gets jammed. To handle this traffic, utilities are forced to turn on expensive, less-efficient backup power plants. Older students analyzed real energy demand charts to identify peak and off-peak windows. This foundational literacy shifted their mindset from simple conservation to active demand flexibility. (Vivid, Credible, Empowering Communication)
  • School Operations and Shifting Institutional Loads: Once student energy teams understood the concept of peak hours, they audited their school buildings to find energy-intensive processes that could be rescheduled. They worked in close collaboration with school custodians and administrators to implement changes.
    • Device-Charging Carts: Many schools had dozens of mobile carts charging hundreds of laptops or tablets simultaneously. Students designed campaigns to ensure these carts were plugged in overnight or early in the morning, rather than mid-afternoon when the regional grid was under maximum stress.
    • HVAC Pre-Cooling and Pre-Heating: Students worked with facilities managers to adjust building automation systems. For example, during warmer months, they might coordinate pre-cooling the school building early in the morning using lower-cost, off-peak energy, then easing off the air conditioning during peak afternoon hours.
    • End-of-Day and Pre-Weekend PowerDowns: At the end of each school day and before long weekends and holiday breaks, students executed building-wide sweeps to unplug heavy auxiliary equipment such as copiers, laminators, and smartboards. This eliminated unnecessary phantom loads during regional peak events.
  • Household Habits: The e-pledge system asked families to commit to specific, time-based behaviors.
    • Appliance Rescheduling: Households pledged to delay running heavy-use appliances—such as dishwashers and clothes dryers—until after 8:00 PM or early in the morning.
    • Time-of-Use Alignment: In areas where utility sponsors had established Time-of-Use pricing, students acted as household translators, helping their parents map out household tasks to align with the cheapest, cleanest energy windows.
    • Thermostat Flexibility: Families pledged to adjust their home thermostats by just one to two degrees during peak utility windows, to help prevent grid failure during extreme weather.

Maintaining Engagement in Subsequent Years

To keep schools engaged and prevent burnout, the PowerSave program and then the EmPowered Schools program structured subsequent years of competition using altered baseline methodologies and scoring rules.

1. How the Baseline was Calculated in Subsequent Years

To ensure fairness, the program did not simply use the successful previous year as the new baseline. Instead, it utilized two primary accounting frameworks.

  • The Static Historical Baseline: The program often locked in the original, pre-program utility baseline (the year before the school joined the initiative). When schools competed in year two or year three, their performance was still compared to that original pre-program baseline. The goal shifted from active reduction to verified persistence of savings.
  • The Peer-Group Controls Model: In advanced utility-sponsored iterations, returning schools were judged against a control group of non-participating similar schools in the district or region. If a returning school continued to use significantly less energy than its non-participating peers—adjusted for weather—they were awarded competitive points.

2. Advanced Tiers

When schools returned to the program in subsequent years, they moved from the rookie track into alumni or advanced tiers. The competition criteria moved from raw percentage drops in energy use toward deeper, more complex behavioral actions. (Building Motivation and Engagement Over Time)

  • Shifting from energy reduction to demand flexibility: First-year schools focused on conservation (e.g., turning items off). Returning schools were trained in load shifting. They competed on their ability to shed energy specifically during a utility's peak demand windows (usually 2:00 PM to 6:00 PM). Students learned to orchestrate peak shedding drills, which were highly valued by utility sponsors and scored heavily on the program's leaderboard.
  • Social Diffusion: Advanced tier schools earned points not just by auditing their own classrooms, but also by acting as sustainability mentors. They earned competitive points by adopting a nearby elementary school and having older students train the younger students. They also earned points by launching community-wide campaigns where students secured signed energy pledges from local businesses and/or households. (Obtaining a Commitment)
  • Institutional Policy Changes: While year one relied on daily habits like checking light switches, subsequent years targeted institutional memory. Returning teams competed by advocating for policy updates within their district, such as securing a formal board-of-education mandate for holiday building shutdowns or codifying a green purchasing policy that barred the school from buying appliances that lacked Energy Star certification.

Replication Framework

To scale the program to hundreds of schools across different states simultaneously, the ASE developed a structured replication framework. This framework treated national deployment not as a series of isolated local projects, but as a standardized, software-supported network.

Phase 1: Utility and State-Level Coalition Building (The Funding Engine)

Before recruiting new schools, ASE first secured regional financial anchors. Because behavioral energy efficiency qualified as a cost-effective resource for utility demand-side management portfolios, ASE partnered with statewide investor-owned utilities (e.g., PSE&G, New Jersey Natural Gas). The utilities funded the program's administration, providing it at no cost to the school districts, which eliminated initial financial barriers to entry.

Phase 2: Digital Infrastructure

Rather than mailing heavy boxes of paper manuals, Empowered Schools delivered digital toolkits to participating regions. Each school received the following.

  • A localized dashboard connected to the national leaderboard.
  • Standardized, print-ready files for physical prompts (switch stickers, door hangers).
  • Animated modules on demand response
  • Quizzes
  • Digital trackers that accepted standardized utility data inputs and tracked each school's progress in real time against national cohorts.

Phase 3: Train-the-Trainer Educator Onboarding

To scale human infrastructure without sending ASE staff to every city, the program ran regional virtual onboarding summits. Teachers were recruited and trained in cohorts how to integrate the energy audits directly into their existing lesson plans.

Addressing Key Barriers

The following two tables show the key barriers to action and how they were addressed (Overcoming Specific Barriers).

Addressing Barriers Faced by Teachers, Custodial/Facilities Personnel

Barrier

How it was addressed

Other priorities; lack of time to do everything

·        Every program activity was aligned with Next Generation science standards and state STEM mandates. Instead of asking teachers to find extra time, the program provided them with turnkey lessons that fulfilled requirements they were already legally mandated to teach.

·        The program required schools to include custodians at the very inception of the Energy Team. The curriculum framed custodians as expert mentors. Students interviewed them when mapping their buildings. Furthermore, the behavioral checklists were explicitly designed to reduce custodial workloads—such as students taking responsibility for unplugging heavy classroom electronics before long weekends so custodians didn't have to check every room themselves.

·        Easy-to-use digital portal that simplified participation

School district bureaucracies

·        The program bypassed bureaucratic gridlock by targeting individual schools, and empowering local champions (teachers and students) rather than waiting for top-down school board mandates.

Measuring Achievements

  • Baseline Establishment: A rolling 12-month pre-program utility billing baseline was constructed for each participating facility. In subsequent years, that baseline was adjusted for changes in the total electric load caused by:
    • Physical classroom additions or campus renovations
    • Changes in overall facility scheduling or building usage hours
    • Independent facility upgrades (such as a pre-scheduled district-wide LED retrofit)
  • Weather Normalization: Starting with utility billing data, energy use during the active program period was adjusted using univariate regression modeling against local Heating Degree Days and Cooling Degree Days to ensure that mild or harsh weather did not skew results.
  • Baseload Analysis: Continuous tracking focused heavily on weekend, holiday, and nighttime baseload drops, which served as direct indicators of successful behavioral shutdowns (e.g., unplugging vending machines, turning off peripheral electronics, and adjusting setbacks).
  • Student Surveys: ASE used standardized, age-appropriate digital surveys administered at the very beginning and end of each school year to measure STEM literacy, environmental self-efficacy, and behavioral persistence (e.g., over school breaks and at home over the course of the school year).
  • E-Pledge Tracking and Household Actions: The e-pledge dashboard required households to select specific, high-impact, verifiable actions, such as:
    • Washing at least two loads of laundry per week in cold water instead of hot.
    • Adjusting the home thermostat by 1 to 2 degrees (e.g., down to 68°F in winter).
    • Swapping out remaining incandescent or CFL bulbs for energy-efficient LEDs.
    • Unplugging video game consoles or secondary TVs when going on vacation to eliminate vampire loads.

      • Because the e-pledge system required users to input their zip codes and select discrete actions, the platform could use regional utility algorithms to automatically calculate the aggregate projected energy savings. The program could then tell a utility sponsor: "In Zone X, 450 households pledged cold-water washing and LED swaps, resulting in an estimated savings of Y Megawatt Hours and Z Therms this quarter."

    Providing Feedback

    The program portal automatically aggregated each school’s inputs, calculated the school's initial state of energy waste, and visualized it on a digital dashboard. In some schools, the local utility sponsor had installed advanced metering infrastructure and could share aggregated interval energy data with the program. The data were automatically fed into the platform, allowing students to look at actual, real-time kWh charts of their school building’s energy use.

    Results

    Over its 30-year operational history from 1996 to 2026, the PowerSave and EmPowered Schools initiatives delivered predictable, utility-verified results.

    As of 2025, the program was engaging roughly 300 to 500 schools and 200,000 students each year. Of these students, roughly 10,000 gained hands-on, direct experience forming core Energy Teams to conduct lighting, HVAC, and appliance plug-load audits in their buildings.

    Each year on average, the program saved 1,667,667 kWh of energy, 685 metric tonnes of CO2 equivalents, and $200,000. By 2026, cumulative savings were 50,000,000 kWh of energy, 20,550 Metric Tonnes, of CO2 equivalents and $6,000,000. The savings would be even greater if the associated changes in home energy use were also considered. Some utility partners noted measurable drops in residential territory energy use when students brought their newly formed energy conservation habits home to their families.

    In 2014, DiMatteo and colleagues published an evaluation of the program in two school districts. In one district, there was significant improvement in energy conservation knowledge and specific behaviors; however, there was no significant improvement in the other district. Information is not publicly available on findings from the student surveys that measured changes in STEM literacy, environmental self-efficacy, and behavioral persistence.
