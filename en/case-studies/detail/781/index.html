FireSmart Canada and FireWise USA

Introduction

As climate change leads to more wildfires, it becomes increasingly important to understand how to best promote fire prevention behaviors. Both the Canadian FireSmart and the American Firewise programs have strong behavioral components. While they also have components that focus on municipal bylaws, provincial/state policies, forestry management practices, and standards for fire-resilient infrastructure and building materials, this case study focuses on the behavioral aspects of these programs. Communities that had achieved active Firewise USA status had enough people taking enough actions that they were on average 12.5% less likely to have experienced a damaging fire.

Background

Note: To minimize site maintenance costs, all case studies on this site are written in the past tense, even if they are ongoing as is the case with this particular program.

The Canadian FireSmart™ program and American Firewise USA program collaborated and co-evolved, which is why this case study is written about both. Both programs focused on areas where wildfires do the most property damage - the wildland-urban interface.

In 1986, following a devastating 1985 wildfire season that destroyed over 1,400 homes across the United States, the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) partnered with the United States Department of Agriculture’s Forest Service and other federal agencies to create a national project addressing wildfire risks in the wildland-urban interface. In 1992, the project’s advisory group formally adopted the term Firewise to describe a property or community that was knowledgeable, prepared, and resilient against wildfires.

The Canadian FireSmart™ program was created in the early 1990s by the Alberta Forest Service in collaboration with provincial public safety, parks, and fire chief associations, to address increasingly frequent and severe wildfires where human development met undeveloped wildland. In 1993, they incorporated the Partners in Protection Association (PiP), a non-profit multidisciplinary coalition that managed the FireSmart brand. It consisted of professionals representing national, provincial, and municipal emergency services, land-use planners, and researchers.

The prevailing belief at the time was that a large, high-intensity wildfire could easily destroy a home because of its size and power—and that nothing could be done to save the house. Dr. Jack Cohen, a scientist with the USDA Forest Service, questioned this assumption. In 1995, using computer and mathematical modeling, he calculated that a fire needed to get within 40 meters (about 130 feet) of a typical wooden wall before its heat could ignite it. He called the area within that distance the Home Ignition Zone (HIZ). He hypothesized that many homes could be saved using a buffer zone approach - clearing vegetation and other combustibles within the HIZ.

In 1996 the US Forest Service launched the Firewise website to share Cohen’s conclusions.

In 1997, Cohen partnered with the Canadian Forest Service and international agencies to conduct the International Crown Fire Modelling Experiment in a remote stretch of boreal forest near Fort Providence, in Canada’s Northwest Territories.

The researchers constructed wooden ‘house walls’ at different distances from plots of forest that they purposely set on fire. They found that if the fire front was over 30 meters (100 feet) from the wooden structures, it was unable to ignite them directly. Instead, the fires spread either by (1) flying embers landing on flammable materials, or (2) travelling along the ground where flammable ground cover permitted.

These field experiments showed that two key factors could protect homes from wildfire: the homes’ building materials, and the management of combustible materials within 30 to 60 meters (100 to 200 feet) of the home.

In 1999, PiP published Protecting your Community from Wildfire, to immediately operationalize the early breakthroughs resulting from this research. It was a landmark technical manual adopted by fire agencies as far away as Australia and New Zealand. It divided the area around a structure into three distinct priority zones based on critical distances associated with ignition physics. It also included a point-scoring assessment system for roofing, siding, and vegetation zones.

That year Cohen published the academic, peer-reviewed proof of the heat-transfer physics involved.

By 2000, other Canadian provinces began formally adopting the FireSmart™ guidelines, and the program became the de facto Canadian standard for wildfire mitigation.

In 2008, the Canadian Council of Forest Ministers (CCFM) asked PiP to scale FireSmart™ to a formal national program. This integrated FireSmart into the broader Canadian Wildland Fire Strategy, officially shifting it from an Alberta-led initiative into a national one. At this point, they had a good idea of the behaviors they wanted to promote. Their information campaigns had been raising public awareness. But the promoted behaviors were not being adopted on private property. CCFM proposed adapting the American Firewise model and grounding it heavily in Community-Based Social Marketing (CBSM) principles to build the Neighbourhood Recognition Program.

That year PiP collaborated with Alberta Sustainable Resource Development to launch FireSmart's first formal CBSM pilot in the Foothills region of Alberta. The pilot used short surveys and face-to-face home hazard assessments to identify and address the psychological and structural barriers that kept people from making the suggested changes on their properties. The pilot increased local vegetation management and structural mitigation compliance.

Prioritizing Audiences

The programs identified four priority audiences.

    1. Homeowners and Residents were the projects’ primary audiences, responsible for managing the immediate Home Ignition Zone.
    2. Neighborhoods and Community Groups to foster collective accountability and norm appeals.
    3. Municipal Governments and Local Planners responsible for zoning, local bylaws, and structural development.
    4. In Canada, a fourth priority audience was Indigenous Communities who helped ensure that risk mitigation plans respected and integrated with traditional knowledge.

    Delivering the Program

    Both FireSmart and FireWise aimed to get people to take low-cost actions that removed combustible materials (like cutting tall grass, cleaning gutters, and pruning trees) and higher cost actions (like replacing roofing or siding), all of which reduced the risk of fire spreading on a property and into a home.

    Instead of warning people about the dangers of wildfires, the two programs promoted wildfire preparedness as a normal part of property maintenance. They broke each property down into distinct, manageable concentric circles, and encouraged residents to start with the inner Zone and gradually work towards managing the zones that were farther from the house.

    Building Engagement Over Time

    Both FireSmart and FireWise used mechanics like those used by game designers to keep players engaged and motivated. (Building Motivation and Engagement Over Time)

    1. They Created a "Levels" System (Progression)

    In a game, you start at ‘level 1’ and work your way to higher levels. The HIZ system functioned the same way by prioritizing proximity:

    • Level 1 (Immediate Zone: 0 to 1.5 meters): This was the most critical, highest-impact area and had to be completely non-combustible. For example, no leaf litter, combustible mulch, wood chips, or firewood next to the home structure.
    • Level 2 (Intermediate Zone: 1.5 to 10 meters): The yard and immediate landscaping had to be fire-resistant. This might involve creating "fuel breaks" (like lawns, driveways, or walkways). It might involve clearing dead vegetation and tree branch pruning. It might also involve planting fire-resistant plants. For example, the FireSmart Plant Program highlighted fire-resistant plants with special tags at participating nurseries. Both programs explicitly supported or actively prioritized using native flora to promote regional ecological balance and biodiversity.
    • Level 3 (Extended Zone: 10 to 30+ meters): The outer property line and forested edges had to be thinned. All coniferous trees and some deciduous trees had to be spaced a minimum distance apart to prevent crowning fires.

    Instead of looking at their entire property and panicking about the scale of the work, homeowners were told to focus only on the first 1.5 meters. Once they completed that zone, they mentally unlocked and progressed to the next level. This overcame the identified barrier of complexity and feeling overwhelmed. (Overcoming Specific Barriers)

    2. They Started with Easy Wins

    Good games hook players with opening stages that are so easy that the players build the confidence to move on to more challenging stages. In a similar manner, both FireSmart and Firewise asked residents to first focus on the Immediate Zone (0 to 1.5 meters). Many tasks in this zone costed nothing and required minimal physical labor—such as sweeping dry leaves off the porch, moving a welcome mat, or shifting a stack of firewood away from the house. These easy wins built a sense of accomplishment and the momentum needed to tackle more difficult and expensive tasks.

    3. They Turned Vague Goals into Specific, Achievable Objectives

    Games are engaging because they give you specific, achievable objectives. both FireSmart and Firewise provided specific objectives for each of the three HIZ zones. This also helped overcome the identified barrier of complexity and feeling overwhelmed. (Overcoming Specific Barriers)

    4. They Provided Visual Feedback

    In video games, clearing an area of enemies or fog provides instant visual gratification. A similar thing happened when a resident looked out the window and saw the cleared and fire-resistant zones around the home. This boosted a sense of control and safety and helped overcome the barrier of feeling overwhelmed. (Feedback; Overcoming Specific Barriers) In addition, seeing the cleared zones around their neighbors’ homes helped residents overcome the identified barrier of existing negative norm appeals. (Norm Appeals; Overcoming Specific Barriers)

    As the two programs co-evolved, the following key components emerged.

    Home Hazard Assessments

    Risk assessments for individual homes have been central to both programs, even though they have always been optional in both Canada and the USA.

    HIZ Self-Assessments

    • Canada: Starting in 1999, PiP provided the first HIZ Self-Assessment Scorecard inside its landmark manual, FireSmart: Protecting Your Community from Wildfire. The scorecard gave homeowners a point system for rating roofing, siding, and vegetation zones.

      The same year, this paper scorecard was digitized into the FireSmart Begins at Home mobile app and further expanded into streamlined provincial online self-assessment portals. By 2020, the mobile app was the centerpiece of local municipal spring mitigation campaigns in such places as Yellowknife and Penticton.

    • USA: With the launch of the national Firewise Communities USA® Recognition Program in 2002, the NFPA developed and began distributing its own paper-based HIZ checklists and self-assessment scorecards. The NFPA then created interactive web-based toolkits hosted by Firewise USA, and various states developed their own apps (e.g. California's Ready for Wildfire app).

    Personalized, Face-to-Face Home Hazard Assessments

    • Canada: In 2008, PiP collaborated with Alberta Sustainable Resource Development to launch FireSmart's first formal CBSM pilot in the Foothills region of Alberta. The pilot offered face-to-face home hazard assessments. The home hazard assessor walked with residents on their properties, drawing their attention to specific immediate vulnerabilities and what they could do to remedy each. For example, the assessor might have pointed out a juniper bush touching a wooden deck that could have acted as a fuse. The solution might have been as simple as pulling up the bush. (Home Visits; Vivid, Personalized, Credible, Empowering Communication)

      Instead of demanding an overnight property overhaul, assessors asked residents to make small, incremental commitments for low-cost, achievable steps—like pruning nearby bushes, cleaning out gutters or moving a firewood pile away from the house. (Building Motivation and Engagement Over Time; Obtaining a Commitment)

      This countered four of the key barriers found from the pre-pilot research: (1) a perception gap & complacency, (2) complexity and feeling overwhelmed, (3) responsibility shifting, and (4) financial and physical costs. <(Overcoming Specific Barriers)

      In 2017, FireSmart began developing and offering more rigorous, professional home assessments, based on the Wildfire Partners initiative in Colorado, USA. With funding from the Forest Resource Improvement Association of Alberta, the program ran five pilots in Whitecourt, the Municipal District of Lesser Slave River, and the Regional Municipality of Wood Buffalo. The trained assessors walked properties with residents and gave them highly customized property vulnerability reports. These assessments were considered the next step to take after using the FireSmart Begins at Home app or completing the self-assessment scorecard.

      In 2018 and 2019 private funders such as Intact Financial Corporation supported development of a Home Partners Program mobile app and online training portal. By 2019, this enabled training of 500 firefighters a year, compared to just 100 previously. Summer students and fire crews used the app to conduct over 350 advanced assessments in the Fort McMurray area alone.

      In 2021, when FireSmart operations were shifted to the Canadian Interagency Forest Fire Centre, the home visits were rebranded as the Advanced Home Assessment Program and assessor training was provided to structural firefighters, municipal staff, and Local FireSmart Representatives (LFRs).

    • USA: From the beginning, Firewise strongly encouraged residents to ask their state forestry agency or local fire protection district for a site assessment. Availability depended on local capacity. Fire staff would use the official Firewise checklist to walk individual properties with homeowners. Over time, NFPA developed a specialized curriculum for professionals: the Assessing Structure Ignition Potential from Wildfire training program. It trained firefighters, foresters, and community leaders to carry out the home assessments. The curriculum was continually updated and had a major modern instructional upgrade in 2017.

    Community Recognition Programs

    • Canada: In 2008, the FireSmart Communities Recognition Program was pitched to the Canadian Council of Forest Ministers as a direct result of the successful Foothills CBSM pilot that year. Participating neighborhoods could gain recognition and special street signs. In 2012, Canadian fire officials adapted the American blueprint for use in Canada. Within a year, the District of Logan Lake, British Columbia became the first community recognized under the Community Recognition Program.

      In 2021, it was rebranded as the Neighbourhood Recognition Program to reflect that the program's success relied on neighbourhood champions and committees, not broader community/city-wide efforts. Participating communities could access recurring annual funding if they met targets at their mandatory yearly work bees. (Financial Incentives)

      The grant money typically covered expenses a volunteer group couldn’t easily handle out-of-pocket. For example, the grants were used to rent industrial woodchippers, pay local contractor fees for hazardous tree removal, cover municipal dump and organic waste disposal fees, purchase safety gear, and buy communal tools that the neighborhood could share for future cleanups.

      To unlock the funding, a community had to have:

      • An active profile in the national portal
      • A valid Neighborhood Wildland Fire Hazard Assessment, conducted by a specialist within the last five years
      • A current Neighborhood Mitigation Plan (three years old or less).

      A community’s grant application had to specify the exact task from their approved mitigation plan the money would fund.

      To receive funding, the neighborhood had to have a motivated resident step up as a Neighbourhood Champion to create a local FireSmart Neighbourhood Committee. This group determined their neighborhood boundaries and registered on the national portal. An LFR or NRP Specialist then guided them through the technical mitigation process.

      The LFR then conducted an extensive walkthrough or drive-by of the area and presented an assessment report to the neighborhood committee showing where the community was most vulnerable to ember ignition and surface fires. The committee then designed a tailored FireSmart Neighbourhood Plan with at least three achievable mitigation actions on communal open spaces and private properties.

      To receive funding, neighborhoods had to demonstrate an annual investment equivalent in local FireSmart efforts of $2 per resident.

    • USA: Early on, the NFPA realized that fire departments could not physically defend every house, and that homeowners had to share responsibility for fire safety and become active partners with local fire agencies. They were also concerned that a purely educational approach was not resulting in enough physical, on-the-ground changes. In response, the Communities Recognition Program was designed to incentivize collective community action and systematically lower the risk profile of an entire community. (Norm Appeals)

      In 2002, the Communities Recognition Program was formally launched with an initial pilot of 12 (later expanding to 14) self-selected neighborhoods across states such as Arizona, California, and Colorado.

      While community pride and norm appeal were themselves powerful drivers, Firewise USA also arranged for Financial Incentives.

      • Insurance Discounts: In many wildfire-prone states (such as California, Colorado, Oregon, and Texas), state insurance commissioners or specific providers offered or mandated premium discounts for homeowners living within a recognized Firewise USA community.
      • Grant Funding: When state and federal agencies distributed highly competitive grants for reducing flammable materials or community safety, a priority was often given to applicants in recognized Firewise sites, because they already had the organizational infrastructure to complete the projects successfully.

      Firewise recognition had to be renewed annually. This prevented mitigation from being treated as a one-time chore. By requiring yearly reporting of actions and investments, the program put wildfire preparedness onto each community’s yearly calendar. (Building Motivation and Engagement Over Time; Prompts)

      To earn and maintain official national recognition, communities had to complete a structured five-step process.

      1. Form a board of local volunteer residents.
      2. Conduct a professional fire risk assessment in collaboration with local fire staff.
      3. Create a multi-year action plan for reducing home ignition risks. These three-year plans prioritized collective projects, such as clearing common areas, organizing neighborhood brush-cleanup days, or optimizing evacuation routes.
      4. Host at least one event a year that was either educational or a physical cleanup Firewise Day.
      5. Invest locally in the program’s success, with a minimum annual financial or volunteer-hour contribution to local mitigation efforts, equivalent to one volunteer hour per dwelling unit.

    Work Bees and Community Workdays

    • Regardless of whether they were called Neighborhood Clean-up Days, Community Workdays, Chipping Days or Work Bees, these gatherings involved a community coming together to clean up flammable areas and meet their program's requirement for residents contributing to the cleanup effort. The collaboration aspect usually involved Chipping Days, assisting vulnerable neighbors, and clearing common areas such as hiking trails, fuel breaks behind homes, and overgrown vegetation blocking evacuation roads.
    • Canada: The 2008 CBSM pilot tested the shift in focus from individual homeowners to the broader neighborhood unit, with local work bees and community events. When residents saw their neighbors actively raking debris, pruning trees, and using shared woodchippers, the sense of acting in isolation dissolved, replaced by positive peer reinforcement. This also led to neighbors talking with each other about the program. (Norm Appeals, Overcoming Specific Barriers; Word of Mouth)

      Starting in 2015, FireSmart offered registered neighborhoods two kinds of community workday funding.

      • The $500 Prep Day Award was designed to spark enthusiasm, provide an easy win, serve as visible proof-of-concept that collective mitigation was achievable (overcome initial friction and inertia) and hook new neighborhoods into the program. It was available to any individual, informal community group, or municipality. There was no need to be formally enrolled yet in FireSmart’s Neighbourhood Recognition Program. There was broad flexibility to fund a local event held between May and November. All one needed to apply was a simple event plan to raise awareness or host a hands-on mitigation day.
      • In contrast, the $750 to $1,000 NRP incentives were designed for more experienced communities and required participating neighborhoods to be enrolled in the NRP system, have an official Hazard Assessment, a valid Neighbourhood Mitigation Plan, a registered Neighbourhood Champion, and an active local FireSmart Committee. Funded activities had to support specific actions listed in the communities’ mitigation plans. New applicants received $1,000, while renewing neighborhoods got $750.
      • Using both tracks ensured there was a place for everyone to start, and a financial carrot to keep them moving forward.ilding Motivation and Engagement Over Time)

      Neighbourhood Champions organized the work bees, based on a provided structure, and documented their neighborhood's collective investment by logging volunteer hours, participation, and in-kind contributions (e.g. donated chainsaws or trucking services). Once the event details and impact metrics were submitted online, the application was reviewed by provincial and national coordinators, culminating in the neighborhood receiving official recognition status, annual incentive grant eligibility, and a formal "FireSmart Neighbourhood" street sign to proudly display.

    • USA: Starting in 2002, the third step in becoming a recognized Firewise Community was to hold an annual Firewise Day, defined as either a public education seminar or a community workday/clean-up day. In 2018, the NFPA retired the public education option and the phrase Firewise Day because too many communities just had an information booth at a local summer barbecue. The day was renamed to require working together to clean up flammable debris through Neighborhood Clean-up Days, Community Workdays, or Chipping Days.

      Firewise USA® communities with a board, community risk assessment, three year action plan, and minimum volunteer contribution could apply for official recognition. They were then given one complimentary heavy metal “Firewise USA® Recognized Site” street sign to mount at the main neighborhood entrance and could purchase additional signs for secondary crossroads. To keep the sign valid, the neighborhood had to re-submit their volunteer hours and update their action plans each year. These signs signaled to visitors, insurers, and neighbors that the street had collectively committed to wildfire safety. (Building Motivation and Engagement Over Time; Norm Appeals, Overcoming Specific Barriers; Word of Mouth Communication)

      Chipping Days involved purchasing or renting a woodchipper and engaging a community to participate in the chipping. As it became increasingly clear that embers and the vegetation surrounding homes were the main reasons structures caught fire, state forestry agencies and local fire departments started buying or renting commercial woodchippers to help their communities. Residents dragged brush, branches, and juniper bushes from their yards to the curb to be safely disposed of. This was a very visible sign of participation and led to neighbors talking with each other about the program. (Norm Appeals, Overcoming Specific Barriers; Word of Mouth)

      In 2013, Wildfire Community Preparedness Day was piloted in Colorado and Texas, without any grants for participants. Even so, the approach was so effective at generating grassroots action that it was launched nationally in 2014. Funding from State Farm Insurance allowed the NFPA to provide $500 grants to neighborhood groups to cover community cleanup tools, chipper rentals, and safety resources. This spurred FireSmart Canada to launch its own version of the program in 2015.

    Youth Outreach and Character Marketing

    • Canada: FireSmart used youth outreach and character marketing to make the topic of wildfire safety more accessible for families and foster long-term cultural change. Ember™ the FireSmart Fox was the official national mascot of the program. Ember focused on the risk of flying embers, and on fire mitigation and resilience. Activities dovetailed with curriculum opportunities from kindergarten to grade 12. Coloring books, interactive story videos, and the Ember’s Kids Home Assessment tool were two popular components. Children learned about fire safety at school then ‘pestered’ their parents to let them lead their own families through a safety checklist and clear fire hazards from their homes. (School Programs that Involve the Family)
    • USA: Firewise also used youth outreach and character marketing for similar reasons. Sparky the Fire Dog® was the official national mascot. Through platforms like Sparky’s School House, the NFPA provided videos, apps, and activities tailored for youth. While Sparky had traditionally focused on responding to structural fires (e.g. escaping a burning building), his branding expanded to include wildfire preparedness. Firewise reinforced Sparky’s advice with messages from Smokey Bear about fire prevention. States and communities often used regional mascots as well, such as Captain Cal in California, and Kaleo the Pueo (a native owl) in Hawaii.

      One of the program's most successful initiatives was its collaboration with the Girl Scouts and Boy Scouts. State forestry agencies (such as the Florida Forest Service) created specific Firewise Patch programs based on an experiential learning model. Participants learned about wildfire behavior and how embers travel. They interviewed local firefighting professionals or mapped the HIZ around their own properties. Then they completed a community service project, like clearing dry brush and pine needles at a scout camp or helping elderly neighbors clear debris from their gutters.

      Firewise transformed scientific data into gamified activities for elementary and middle school students. Its Firewise Detective approach gave participants checklists to assess their own homes, in the spirit of a scavenger hunt. (School Programs that Involve the Family) NFPA also distributed bilingual coloring and activity books featuring word searches, mazes, and spot-the-hazard games.

    Accompanying Mass Media Campaigns and Promotion

    • Canada: Each year before the wildfire season peaked, FireSmart ran multi-platform media campaigns across television, billboards, radio, and social media. (Mass Media; Vivid, Credible, Empowering Communication)
      • Please Don't Feed the Wildfires was a national, bilingual campaign that played on the “don’t feed wild animals” theme. It reframed combustibles as food that fuels a wildfire and urged residents to starve the fire before it arrived.
      • Our Part, Your Part was a regional/provincial campaign designed to overcome the barrier of responsibility shifting. It contrasted the intense, dangerous work of wildland firefighters with the relatively easy and safe work (e.g., clearing gutters or raking leaves) that residents were asked to do. It highlighted the need for shared responsibility, and for residents to do their easier part so firefighters could safely do theirs. (Norm Appeals; Overcoming Specific Barriers)

      The Get FireSmart podcast and magazine featured accounts of communities that had survived major fires because their residents had adopted the behaviors promoted by FireSmart. (Vivid, Credible, Empowering Communication)

      FireSmart also encouraged local volunteers and fire departments to help promote the program and supported them with materials such as flyers and social media graphics. They gave municipalities, real estate associations, insurance companies, and indigenous communities a social media handbook with pre-packaged messages, enabling local authorities to send out trusted communications adapted to their local fire risk conditions. They also provided branded drink coasters to local pubs, BBQs, and community legions to inspire casual, peer-to-peer conversations about fire safety. (Word of Mouth Communication)

    • In 2020, FireSmart launched a free, one-hour, self-paced online training course called FireSmart 101. It was designed to equip ordinary citizens with the self-efficacy needed to protect their properties and champion localized community resilience. The course became a prerequisite to becoming a certified FireSmart Ambassador, an LFR, or an NRP Specialist. Provinces like British Columbia and Alberta tied municipal funding allocations to the widespread promotion and local completion of FireSmart 101. The course was also customized to train thousands of brokers and agents to properly evaluate property risks.

    • USA: In its early years, the NFPA and its partners distributed physical toolkits containing educational booklets, brochures, and posters that explained fire behavior. The late '90s and early 2000s marked a shift from telling individuals to read a brochure to challenging entire neighborhoods to organize.

      The launch of Firewise.org in 1997 created a central repository for media materials, allowing local fire departments and state forestry agencies to download radio scripts, print-ready public service announcements, and boilerplate press releases. The 2002 launch of the Firewise Communities/USA Community Recognition Program added a competitive, badge-of-honor element to the marketing strategy. The NFPA provided TV and radio stations in high-risk zones with professional B-roll and localized data so local news anchors could spread the Firewise message during peak fire seasons. (Building Motivation and Engagement Over Time; Mass Media)

      By 2017, the NFPA realized technical terms like "mitigation" and "home hardening" felt clinical, expensive, and intimidating to the average homeowner. The program was rebranded with a simplified, modern logo accompanied by a clear, action-oriented national tagline: "Residents Reducing Wildfire Risks." "Mitigation" became "wildfire risk reduction", and "home hardening" became "fire-resistant construction."

      Starting in 2018, with the rise of social media and a growing cultural preference for visual, scannable content, Firewise rolled out highly visual, plug-and-play digital campaign kits. Local volunteers were given printable doorhangers with a simple checklist of prevention actions on the front side. The back of the doorhangers explained why these actions were important and provided a QR code or web link to a localized landing page where residents could schedule a free wildfire home assessment from their local fire department. The bottom included a blank white space where local leaders could handwrite a personal note or stamp the local fire marshal's direct phone number.

    The Programs Continued to Evolve

    • Canada: As of 2026, FireSmart was operating in hundreds of recognized neighborhoods in all Canadian provinces and territories. It had been streamlined into three separate delivery pillars for homeowners, neighborhoods, and communities.
    • USA: As of 2026, Firewise was operating in over 2,200 active communities. While the program was technically available for adoption across all 50 states, it was most active in a handful of Western and fire-prone states.

    Tapping Into Motivators

    Here’s how the two programs tapped into their audiences’ key motivators.

    • Higher building survival rate: Both programs placed survival stories in magazines, podcasts, and social media. As one example, they showed side-by-side photos of a home that survived a fire and a neighbouring one that didn’t, featuring the resident of the one that survived explaining the Firewise / FireSmart steps they had taken. (Financial Incentives; Vivid, Credible, Empowering Communication)
    • Sense of control and peace of mind: Both Firewise and FireSmart emphasized that 90% of homes lost to wildfires are lit by wind-borne sparks flying kilometers ahead of the fires, and that residents could avoid that scenario by managing the combustible materials in their yard. The steps to take could seem overwhelming, blocking action, so the programs broke these into small, highly achievable steps. (Building Motivation and Engagement Over Time)
    • Strengthened community ties and a collective sense of pride and shared safety: Both programs had neighborhood recognition plans with special street signs highlighting a community’s commitment. Both programs required participating neighborhoods to have high visibility neighborhood action days. They relied on communications from trusted local messengers, outlined what was expected, and highlighted local participation. (Norm Appeals; Obtaining a Commitment; Personalized, Credible, Empowering Communication)
    • Home maintenance and landscaping co-benefits: Both Firewise and FireSmart emphasized that the actions they promoted contributed significantly to home maintenance and aesthetic landscaping improvement. Rather than telling people to strip their yards bare, they used beautifully illustrated landscaping guides to reframe fire safety as a modern landscaping upgrade. (Norm Appeals; Vivid, Personalized, Credible, Empowering Communication)

    Overcoming Barriers

    The following table summarizes the key barriers to action and how each was addressed.

    Barrier

    How it was addressed

    Many people thought their property’s fire risk was low and that they had already done enough.

    ·        Used vivid, local imagery showing common gaps (Vivid, Personalized, Credible, Empowering Communication)

    ·        Provided free personalized Home Hazard Assessments (Home Visits)

    Taking stock of their homes’ situation and the steps to take could seem overwhelming.

    ·        Broke the steps into small doable chunks that built momentum.

    Many thought that protection from wildfires was the exclusive responsibility of the municipal government, local fire departments, or water bombers.

    ·        Emphasized shared responsibility and how embers ignited individual yards.

    Many resisted high-cost structural retrofits like replacing asphalt shingles or vinyl siding with fire-resistant materials.

    ·        Emphasized starting with less expensive steps, including the non-combustible zone.

    ·        Offered local incentives such as the Conifer Tree Replacement Program and Chipper Day funding and collaborated with the insurance industry to offer premium incentives for those who acted. (Financial Incentives)

    If immediate neighbors didn’t adopt the recommended actions to reduce fire risk, people were discouraged from acting, thinking their own effort would be a waste of time and money if the neighbor’s property caught fire.

    ·        Established neighborhood recognition plans with special street signs highlighting a community’s commitment. (Norm Appeals; Obtaining a Commitment)

    ·        Required participating neighborhoods to have high visibility neighborhood action days. (Norm Appeals)

    Financing the Program

    Canada: FireSmart was funded through a mix of federal, provincial, and municipal grants.

    • Provincial Funding: BC's Community Resiliency Investment (CRI) program was allocating approximately $60 million annually to FireSmart initiatives.
    • Contingency Funds: In 2024, the Alberta government designated $2 billion for disaster contingency, a portion of which supported proactive FireSmart mitigation to reduce future suppression costs.
    USA: Financial information is not publicly available for Firewise.

    Measuring Achievements

    Canada

    The FireSmart program was in a good position to measure behavior change and risk reduction. The baseline property assessment was followed up by a second assessment one to three years later. Success was to be defined as a statistically significant downward trajectory in risk scores across participating neighborhoods. However, the resulting data have not been publicly reported.

    In 2017, FireSmart launched an online survey that, among other things, asked the degree to which the respondent participated in FireSmart activities. Of the 62,867 people who viewed the survey invitations, 3,180 people began the survey, and 2,435 completed it (an overall completion rate, from invitation to survey completion, of 5.1%.) There was neither a baseline nor a control group to compare against, and it is unclear from this information what impacts were from the program itself rather than from external influences.

    USA

    In November 20026, when this case study was published, the strongest evidence to date of behavior changes and risk reduction resulting from the Firewise / FireSmart approach came from Timothy Foreman’s presentation Community Programs Support Wildfire Risk Reduction at the European Geosciences Union General Assembly 2025. Foreman used a differences-in-differences statistical design to directly compare historical wildfire data for California communities that actively participated in the Firewise USA program against structurally and environmentally similar communities that did not participate. At the time of writing (November 2026) the presentation is currently only publicly available as an Executive Summary; it has not yet been published as a peer-reviewed article.

    Each Firewise community had to submit a renewal application every year documenting its ongoing mitigation actions and the volunteer hours or monetary wildfire mitigation investments per dwelling unit. However, this included some routine maintenance activities like mowing a lawn, and there was neither a baseline nor a control group to compare against. The financial investments / uptake of renovation rebates could have acted as an indicator of the desired behaviors, but again there was neither a baseline nor a control group to compare against. It is not clear from this information what impacts were from the program itself.

    Providing Feedback

    When a resident looked out the window and saw the cleared and fire-resistant zones around the home (individual feedback).

    In addition, seeing the cleared and fire-resistant zones around their neighbors’ homes provided community feedback about participation. Similarly, community participation signage, high visibility neighborhood action days, and communications from trusted local messengers highlighted local participation (group feedback).

    Results

    A number of solid studies show that if a household / property / community was Firewise or FireSmart ‘compliant’ (had taken the steps recommended by these programs) there was a dramatic reduction in fire risk and likely damage. However, most of these studies did not assess the number of households taking these steps as a result of the programs themselves.

    Canada

    Due to a lack of baselines and control groups, it is not clear what behavior changes and associated program impacts were a result of the program itself.

    That said, the program reported an exceptional return on investment:

    • Vegetation Management: This was the most cost-effective measure, with a Benefit-Cost Ratio of 32:1 in high-hazard areas.
    • Structure Hardening: Non-combustible construction retrofits provided a Benefit-Cost Ratio of 14:1.
    • National Impact: For every $1 invested in proactive FireSmart measures, the potential savings in future recovery and suppression costs ranged from $4 to $30.

      • Notes

      The Most Receptive Regions Had Experienced a Wildfire Recently

      In their paper From Catastrophe to Caution: The Effect of Wildfires on Community Hazard Mitigation Investments, Liao and colleagues report that when a wildfire impacted at least 1% of a local ZIP code's population, active participation in Firewise surged by 171% compared to the baseline average. Concurrently, the community's voluntary out-of-pocket investment in local mitigation jumped by 34%. They also found additional barriers for racial and ethnic minorities.

      Innovation

      Both Firewise and FireSmart reframed wildfire mitigation from a technical forestry problem and top-down government responsibility into social, collective lifestyle habits with grass-roots community ownership.

      FireSmart was a trailblazer in creating a singular, unified interagency group (Partners in Protection) that broke down silos between provincial forest services, local fire chiefs, and municipal planners. Their 1999 manual, “Protecting your Community from Wildfire”, became a global gold standard used to model programs around the world. Canada has also been a leader in deeply integrating Indigenous communities and traditional ecological knowledge into its national mitigation strategies.

      Replicability

      The approach has proven its replicability. As of 2026, Firewise was operating in over 2,200 active communities, and FireSmart was operating in hundreds of recognized neighborhoods in all Canadian provinces and territories. The approach is sound across North America, and designed to be executed locally, removing the typical bureaucratic and financial frictions that too often kill macro-level safety initiatives.

      Five core factors drive this high replicability.

      1. Modular, Geometry-Based Standardization. Wildfire behavior is highly complex, but both programs broke ember mitigation down into simple steps that could be taken within the HIZ.

      2. The "Local Champion" Model (Co-opting existing infrastructure). Neither program required a massive, newly funded federal or provincial agency to deploy staff to individual front yards. Instead, they used a highly scalable plug-and-play administrative design.

      • They leveraged preexisting local infrastructure—such as neighborhood homeowners’ associations, local volunteer fire departments, municipal staff, or First Nations community leaders.
      • By training a handful of local volunteers to become LFRs or Firewise Committee Leaders, the programs turned everyday citizens into local delivery agents.

      3. Open-Source Templates and Toolkits. A major barrier to local government replication is often the time and cost required to draft municipal policies, hazard assessment forms, and public educational assets. Firewise and FireSmart eliminated this hurdle through open-source templates and toolkits.

      • They provided open-access, ready-made, professionally designed home assessment forms, landscaping guides, and action plan templates.
      • If a small, rural town with zero budget wanted to become fire-adapted, it didn't have to hire expensive consulting firms to draft a strategy. It simply downloaded the programs’ blueprints, attached their town logo, and started working.

      4. Low Barrier to Entry with Tiered Engagement. If these programs had mandated that every house must immediately buy a $15,000 metal roof to participate, replication would have ground to a halt. Instead, the programs were structured around incremental, behavior-driven goals.

      • Low Entry Cost: Initial community recognition focused on low-cost or free sweat equity—raking pine needles, cleaning gutters, trimming low tree branches, and hosting a community Chipping Day.
      • Scaling with Capacity: Once a community had engaged enough participants it could incrementally move up the ladder toward higher-cost structural retrofits, leveraging municipal grants (like BC's Community Resiliency Investment fund) as its capacity grew.

      5. Peer-to-Peer Accountability. Both Firewise and FireSmart successfully channeled community identity into wildfire safety via street signs, neighborhood certificates, and public recognition, turning wildfire mitigation into a shared neighborhood standard rather than an enforced municipal chore.

      Adaptability to Other Behaviors

      The overall approach demonstrated by FireSmart and Firewise can be readily adapted for other community challenges where the individual actions taken by community members determine their collective risk.

      The approach has already been expanded to volcanic and flood hazard management to create all-hazard resilient communities. The following are some other potential behavior areas.

      • Invasive species and pest management
      • Watershed and water conservation
      • Human-wildlife conflict mitigation
      • Neighborhood energy resilience
