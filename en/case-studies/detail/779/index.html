EU Energy Labeling Program

Introduction

The EU Energy Labelling Program has been a cornerstone of European climate policy designed to pull the market toward more energy-efficient appliances and equipment. The program began in the early 1990s, with the first labels for refrigerators introduced in 1994. Since then, it has expanded to cover over 30 product groups, including household appliances, lighting, and industrial equipment.

The program framed the purchase of more energy-efficient products in terms of long-term cost savings, long-term value, and total cost of ownership, and made it easy and safe to buy energy-efficient products. Growing demand spurred manufacturers to develop, manufacture and sell products that were increasingly energy efficient. By 2017 there were so many products in the A category and so few in the other categories that the program had to reassign (rescale) the ratings on the labels. The program is widely cited as one of the most cost-effective public policies in history due to its low administrative overhead relative to the massive private-sector savings it triggered.

Background

Note: To minimize site maintenance costs, all case studies on this site are written in the past tense, even if they are ongoing as is the case with this particular program.

The program originated in the early 1990s as the European Union worked to combine diverse national labeling systems into a single framework to support the European Single Market. Energy consumption data (e.g., kWh/year) was proving insufficient for consumer decision-making; shoppers needed a relative benchmark to understand if a product was good or bad compared to its peers. The European Commission launched the program’s legal framework, Directive 92/75/EEC, in 1992, making energy labels compulsory for household appliances like refrigerators and freezers.

The European Union's energy policy aimed to ensure a sustainable, secure, and affordable energy supply. The program consisted of two pillars:

  • Ecodesign Directive (2009/125/EC) set mandatory minimum energy performance standards. Products that did not meet these requirements were banned from the EU market.
  • Energy Labeling Regulation (EU 2017/1369) required manufacturers to provide a visual A-G rating that helped consumers compare the energy efficiency of products.

These two pillars complemented each other. Ecodesign legislated the worst products out of the market by setting minimum standards. In contrast, the labelling initiative relied on voluntary market pull to boost demand for increasingly energy-efficient products, thereby incentivizing manufacturers to develop and sell products that were increasingly energy efficient.

Getting Informed

From 1991 to 1993, researchers at the University of Leiden tested five different label formats, including:

  • Numerical scales (1–5)
  • Star-based systems
  • Alphabetical scales (A–G)

Consumers preferred a combination of an A–G alphabetical scale and a color-coded traffic light scheme (green to red) because it minimized cognitive load and enabled quick comparisons at the point of purchase.

Prioritizing Audiences

1. Primary Audience: residential and business consumers

The most direct audience for the label was the individual European consumer.

Residential Consumers: The label was designed for the average shopper.

Professional and Business Buyers: This audience often bought in bulk. Small businesses and professional users (e.g., hotel owners buying laundry equipment or office managers buying monitors) used the label to calculate the Total Cost of Ownership.

2. Secondary Audiences

Manufacturers & Suppliers

  • Product Designers & Engineers: By setting relatively empty top tiers, the program created a prestige target for R&D teams.
  • Marketing Departments: Manufacturers used the energy rating as a unique selling proposition in advertising, essentially doing the program’s promotional work for it.

Retailers & Dealers

  • Brick-and-Mortar Retailers: Shop floor staff were targeted for training programs so they could better explain the savings to customers.
  • E-commerce Platforms: Online dealers were targeted through specific digital labeling requirements, ensuring that the energy rating was visible next to the price tag.

    • Institutional Audience

    • Public authorities in the EU were spending roughly 14% of GDP on goods and services. The energy label targeted these bodies by providing a ready-made, legally defensible criterion for green public procurement. Instead of having to write complex technical specifications for energy use, procurement officers could simply mandate that all equipment purchased for schools or government offices must be "Class B or higher."

    Delivering the Program

    The main behavior change tool used was the now-iconic label that manufacturers had to put on their products.

    The program used a combined letter-and-color-coded scale that highlighted the most efficient products with a dark green A and identified the least efficient ones with a bright red G. This avoided the issue of people speaking different languages in different member countries. Further, consumers were pre-conditioned to associate green and A with good/safe and red with bad/warning.(Vivid, Credible, Empowering Communication) The labels prompted consumers to purchase the products with lower long-term costs and helped establish this as a normal shopping approach.(Financial Incentives, Norm Appeals, Prompts).

    The labels made it easy for buyers to quickly compare the energy-efficiency of alternative products. This addressed the barrier of consumer information overload when comparing technical specs. Research showed that the color-coded A–G scale allowed consumers to make a green decision in less than six seconds. (Overcoming Specific Barriers)

    For industrial and business buyers, the labels provided a standardized verification tool that led to faster decision-making that took long-term operational costs into account. Companies working to meet environmental, social, and governance targets saved time by using the labels as verified data points when completing related reports. Government purchasing agents could specify products with an acceptable efficiency class, without having to provide pages of technical specifications. (Overcoming Specific Barriers)

    The labels also made it easier for sales associates to promote energy-efficient products. The labels provided an objective, easy-to-point-to third-party endorsement of how efficient each product was. Salespeople could more easily move a conversation about short-term cost to one about longer-term savings (e.g. "this fridge pays for itself in three years.") This shifted the interaction to a consultative sales approach rather than a high-pressure one. (Vivid, Credible, Empowering Communication)

    Selecting Product Groups

    The Commission identified priority product groups with high saving potential. At first, the program focused on large domestic appliances, like refrigerators, freezers, and washing machines. This helped it to quickly achieve public visibility and relevance.

    The strategy was that increased demand for increasingly energy-efficient products would spur manufacturers to develop, manufacture and sell products that were increasingly energy efficient. (Financial Incentives for the manufacturers). And it worked. By 2017 there were so many products in the A category and so few in the other categories that it was hard to tell the most efficient products from the good but less efficient ones. The program introduced A+ and A++ classes for cold appliances in 2003/2004. In 2010, these were extended to all product categories and an A+++ class was introduced as well. These additional classes proved confusing. In 2021, the program returned to the simple, strict A-G scale. All products were rescaled, leaving the Class A empty. This allowed for future innovation and helped push manufacturers toward even greater efficiency.

    For each product category, implementation involved a multi-step regulatory process.

    • Preparatory Studies: Detailed market and technical research
    • Consultation Forum: Formal meetings with industry experts and civil society
    • Implementation: Starting in 2017, Regulation (EU) 2017/1369 enabled subsequent regulations to be adopted as "Delegated Acts" or "Implementing Measures," which meant they applied across all participating countries, without having to create a new law in each country.
    • Product Surveillance:National authorities were responsible for testing products to ensure they matched their labels and met the minimum standards.

    Promotion

    The program framed the purchase of more energy-efficient products in terms of long-term cost savings, long-term value, and total cost of ownership. It also associated these goods with premium quality and social responsibility.

    In 2020, to ensure the transition to rescaled labels was understood, the EU funded two large-scale communication initiatives.

    • Label 2020: Supported retailers and consumers in transitioning to the new A–G labels through training and point-of-sale materials.
    • BELT: helped consumers calculate the total cost of ownership rather than just the purchase price

    Retailers began using energy label ratings as a filter on their websites. This influenced sorting behavior — consumers began frequently filtering search results by "Energy Class A or higher," which removed less efficient models from view irrespective of price. (Prompts; Overcoming Specific Barriers)

    Product Registry

    In 2019 the EU launched the European Product Registry for Energy Labelling (EPREL). Manufacturers had to register all products requiring an energy label before they could be sold in the EU market. Market surveillance authorities then had immediate access to technical documentation in the EPREL database. Consumers could scan QR codes on product labels to read the detailed product information.

    Addressing Key Barriers

    The following table shows the key barriers to action and how they were addressed (Overcoming Specific Barriers).

    Barrier

    How it was addressed

    Consumer information overload when comparing technical specs of alternative products

    ·        The label provided a cognitive shortcut. Research showed that the color-coded A–G scale allowed consumers to make a green decision in less than six seconds.

    ·        Retailers began using energy label ratings as a filter on their websites.

    Added workload when government, industrial and business buyers looked for more energy-efficient products or specified them in tenders

    ·        Buyers could now look for products in a specific efficiency class, and they could specify them in tenders without having to provide pages of technical specifications..

    Measuring Achievements

    Impacts were tracked annually following the Ecodesign Impact Accounting guidelines.

    • Sales and Stock Data tracked how many units were in use across Europe.
    • An EcoReport Tool calculated total energy savings, greenhouse gas emissions, and consumer expenditures.
    • Real-World Testing compared laboratory energy use results with actual consumer energy use patterns.

    Financing the Program

    The budget for the labelling program was strikingly low because most technical development and physical labeling costs were shouldered by business. The public budget covered administration, policymaking, promotion, and market surveillance.

    Total Annual Public Budget: About $11.8 million USD (€10 million).

    Budget Allocation:

    • DG Energy (Administrative): the equivalent of ~$5.9 million $US for staff, preparatory studies, and the management of the EPREL database.
    • Support & Promotion (e.g., Label 2020, BELT): ~$3.5 million $US for consumer awareness campaigns and retailer training.
    • Product Surveillance & Enforcement: ~$2.4 million $US across participating countries for lab testing and compliance checks.

    Results

    Across recent VHK Ecodesign Impact Accounting (EIA) Annual Reports (such as the 2021–2024 reports), total combined primary energy savings across the EU are reported at roughly 1,017 to 1,072 TWh per year.

    The 2026 report from the European Commission Evaluators estimated that the labelling program accounted for roughly 27% of total program savings. That represents 275 to 289 TWh of primary annual energy savings per year across the EU.

    Roughly 55,084 kWh were saved per $US spent by the European Union administration. The program is widely cited as one of the most cost-effective public policies in history due to its low administrative overhead.

    • The government’s role was primarily as a referee and information-provider, rather than a direct funder of technology. It triggered billions of dollars in private-sector R&D without requiring direct subsidies.
    • The EU developed an EPREL digital registry with streamlined documentation, reducing the time required for compliance monitoring.
    • Once a label regulation passed for a product group, it applied to all 27 member states and millions of units sold annually, spreading the initial administrative cost across a massive volume of energy savings.

    Innovation

    The program has served as a catalyst for industrial innovation. For example, the energy consumption of refrigerators was reduced by over 60% since the labelling scheme began.

    The program also demonstrated the value of having a high-visibility status tier that spurred manufacturers to go beyond minimum requirements.

    The 2021 dynamic rescaling was particularly innovative. Leaving Class A empty created a technological vacuum. Top-tier products were downgraded to Class C or D, sparking a race to earn the new Class A rating.

    The program’s digital integration is also innovative. Linking the QR code to the EPREL database created a self-policing system. Regulators, consumers, and competitors could all access the same product performance information, reducing greenwashing.

    Replicability

    A similar system is being used in dozens of countries such as Brazil and India.

    • In 2026, Brazil also ended its "A+, A++, and A+++" rankings for refrigerators, moving back to a simplified A, B, C scale.
    • India is now recalibrating its 1–5 star ratings as more efficient models enter the market.

    The EU model is appealing to replicate because it offers efficiencies.

    • Language-Neutral Design: Labels that rely on pictograms rather than text can be used across borders with no translation costs, reducing administrative costs.
    • International Test Standards: Labels that meet international standards (e.g. of the International Electrotechnical Commission) enable manufacturers to avoid paying for expensive duplicate tests when expanding to other countries that use the same protocols.
    • The Central Database (EPREL) and QR codes enable regulators to conduct digital product surveillance instead of manual inspections of every shop floor.

    Adaptability to Other Behaviors

    In June 2025, the EU launched a Repairability Index for smartphones and tablets, using a similar approach as the energy label.

    Some EU countries are also now also using a Nutri-Score to promote healthier food choice, using a similar approach as the energy label.

    It is also expected that many other products will start using digital passports based on a central database and QR codes.

    The labelling approach is easy to adapt to other behaviors.

    • All you need is a new standardized scoring grid – the labelling approach stays the same.
    • The use of icons avoids the need for language translation and associated costs.
    • The approach of using delegated acts means that new products can be added without requiring new laws – all that is needed is a technical annex. This makes the approach much simpler and easier for policy managers to work with.
